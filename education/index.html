Nigerian Educational System: Its Curriculum and Implementation. National Policy as It Relates to Education and Early Childhood Education Summary This paper comprehensively examines the complex dynamics of curriculum development and implementation within the Nigerian educational system, with particular emphasis on early childhood education. Through systematic analysis of current policy shifts, historical contexts, and implementation challenges, the study reveals significant discrepancies between policy intentions and practical outcomes. The recent reversal of the mother-tongue instruction policy exemplifies the tension between pedagogical ideals and systemic constraints in a multilingual society. Findings indicate that persistent challenges—including inadequate funding, insufficient teacher training, poor infrastructure, and linguistic diversity—consistently undermine educational quality. Drawing on empirical evidence and theoretical frameworks, this paper argues for a balanced, context-sensitive approach that acknowledges Nigeria's linguistic diversity while addressing practical implementation barriers. The study concludes with evidence-based recommendations for developing a sustainable, effective educational system that respects cultural heritage while preparing students for global participation, emphasizing the need for phased implementation, strategic teacher development, and increased resource allocation to bridge existing policy-implementation gaps. Key Words: Nigeria education policy, curriculum implementation, early childhood education, mother-tongue instruction, educational inequality, language policy, teacher development, educational funding 1 Introduction The Nigerian educational system, one of the largest in Africa, operates within a framework established by the National Policy on Education (NPE), a comprehensive blueprint designed to use education as a tool for national development (Adeoye et al., 2023). Despite this structured policy guidance and numerous curricular reforms over decades, the system is characterized by a profound and persistent chasm between its ambitious goals and its tangible outcomes. This disconnect is starkly illustrated by the nation's learning outcomes, which consistently fall below expectations, and its unenviable status of having over ten million out-of-school children. These challenges are particularly acute in the critical domain of early childhood education (ECE), where the foundational skills for lifelong learning are established, making the analysis of curriculum implementation not just an academic exercise but a national imperative. A quintessential example of the tension between policy intent and practical reality is the recent, controversial reversal of the mother-tongue instruction policy in primary schools. Initially mandated by the NPE to enhance cultural identity and cognitive development in early years (Obiweluozor, 2015), the policy was rescinded merely three years after its introduction, with authorities citing poor academic results in regions that had adopted it. However, a closer examination suggests that this failure was not inherent to the pedagogical principle itself, but rather a consequence of the very systemic deficits that plague the broader educational landscape, including "poor-quality teaching, inadequate materials, low pay for teachers and numerous strikes" (Ogunbowale et al., 2025; Usman, 2020). This pattern of implementation failure extends beyond language policy, permeating the entire educational structure, with early childhood education being disproportionately affected. The ECE sector, as outlined in the NPE, suffers from chronic underfunding (Obiweluozor, 2015), insufficiently trained educators (Aguh & Olutola, 2022), and significant urban-rural disparities in access and quality (Aguh & Olutola, 2022). These systemic barriers collectively undermine the curriculum's potential, creating a situation where policy provisions for foundational learning, cultural integration, and equitable access remain largely theoretical rather than operational realities. Therefore, this term paper argues that the primary impediment to educational quality in Nigeria is not fundamentally a deficit of policy, but a chronic failure to address the systemic and strategic constraints on implementation (Tukurah, 2021; Sule, 2023). Through an analysis of historical policy evolution, the current state of curriculum delivery, and the specific challenges within early childhood education, this study will demonstrate that sustainable progress requires a context-sensitive approach that prioritizes overcoming practical barriers—such as resource allocation, teacher development, and robust monitoring—to finally bridge the gap between the NPE's aspirations and the classroom's reality. 2.1 What is Curriculum and Curriculum Development A curriculum constitutes the foundational framework of an educational system, defined as the "total learning experiences of individuals not only in school but society as well" (Kranthi, 2017, p. 1). It serves as an academic plan, articulating what students should know and be able to do, and provides the structure for teachers to achieve these outcomes. Curriculum development is the systematic and purposeful process through which this framework is created and improved. It is a planned, progressive procedure aimed at implementing positive changes within the educational system to respond to societal and labour market demands (Kranthi, 2017). The process of curriculum development is cyclical and multi-step, progressing from planning and articulation to implementation and evaluation. It begins with a committee assessing needs, identifying trends, and formulating a program philosophy. This is followed by the articulation of grade-level goals and objectives, the identification of instructional resources, and the development of aligned assessment instruments. After implementation, the cycle concludes with ongoing evaluation and updating to ensure the program's continued effectiveness and relevance (Kranthi, 2017). This structured process ensures the curriculum is a coherent, articulated progression of learning rather than a static document. 2.2 What are the challenges of Implementing education curriculum in Nigeria? The implementation of educational curricula in Nigeria faces significant challenges that create a gap between policy and classroom reality. A primary obstacle is chronic underfunding. Nigeria allocates only about 7% of its national budget to education, which is substantially below the UNESCO recommendation of 15-20% (Odigwe & Owan, 2019). This financial shortfall directly leads to inadequate teaching materials, poor infrastructure, and a lack of support for teachers, making it difficult to execute even well-designed curricula effectively. 2.2.1 Inadequate Funding Chronic underfunding is a fundamental barrier to effective curriculum implementation. Nigeria's allocation of approximately 7% of its national budget to education falls far short of the UNESCO recommendation of 15-20% (Odigwe & Owan, 2019). This financial constraint manifests in inadequate teaching materials, dilapidated infrastructure, and insufficient support for teacher development. The consequence is that even well-designed curricula cannot be properly implemented without the necessary investment in these enabling conditions. 2.2.2 Teacher Quality and Development Issues The critical role of teachers in curriculum delivery is undermined by inadequate training, poor remuneration, and high turnover rates. This instability, including a heavy reliance on temporary staff, prevents the consistent application of the curriculum and the development of teaching expertise (Redding & Nguyen, 2020). Furthermore, teachers are often excluded from the curriculum development process, creating a disconnect between policy and classroom practice (Ezewuzie et al., 2025). When educators are expected to implement a curriculum they had no part in designing, and without adequate training, implementation fidelity suffers. 2.2.3 Infrastructure and Technological Gaps The physical and technological environment of learning presents another major implementation barrier. Many Nigerian schools lack basic facilities, and the digital divide is particularly pronounced. Challenges such as inadequate infrastructure, unstable power, and a shortage of computers and internet access make it difficult to implement curriculum components related to digital literacy and modern skills development (Nwuke & Yellowe, 2025). This technological deficit prevents students from gaining the competencies required by a modern curriculum. 2.2.4 What is early childhood education? 2.3 Early Childhood Education and Language Policy Early childhood education in Nigeria is a critical foundation for learning, yet it is where significant language policy challenges emerge. The sector is marked by a complex struggle to implement pedagogically sound language approaches amidst practical constraints. A central issue is the tension between the recognized value of mother-tongue education and the formidable obstacles to its execution, a conflict highlighted by recent policy shifts. 2.3.1 Policy Instability and Implementation Gap A primary challenge is the inconsistency and weak application of language policy. The reversal of the 2022 National Language Policy, which mandated instruction in the language of the immediate community from early childhood to Primary Six, demonstrates this instability. Such policy fluctuations undermine long-term planning. Furthermore, there is a significant gap between policy documents and their execution, as government organs have historically only paid "lip service to their implementation" (Adeniyi & Ayodele, 2024). This lack of consistent and committed application prevents any language policy from taking root effectively. 2.3.2 Logistical and Capacity Constraints The practical implementation of mother-tongue education faces severe logistical hurdles. Many Nigerian languages lack standardized orthographies and teaching materials, making it impossible to use them as mediums of instruction (Iwuchukwu, 2021). Compounding this is a critical shortage of teachers trained to teach in indigenous languages, especially at the early childhood level. This "inadequate teaching staff and instructional resources" creates a fundamental barrier to putting any language policy into practice (Iwuchukwu, 2021). Without the necessary materials and professionally equipped teachers, policies remain theoretical. 2.3.3 Societal Attitudes and Perceptions Negative societal attitudes towards indigenous languages present a deep-seated challenge. Many parents and communities perceive instruction in local languages as a barrier to their children's acquisition of English, which is seen as the language of economic and social mobility (Jegede, 2024). This perception leads to resistance against mother-tongue policies. The historical colonial legacy that favored English has devalued indigenous languages in the public consciousness, creating a socio-cultural environment that is often hostile to policies aimed at revitalizing them (Adeniyi & Ayodele, 2024). This public resistance severely hampers the acceptance and success of language initiatives. Together, these factors—policy instability, logistical failures, and negative societal attitudes—create a formidable barrier to establishing an effective and sustainable language policy in Nigeria's early childhood education sector. 2.4 Policy implementation in pre-primary education in Nigeria The implementation of policy in Nigeria's pre-primary education sector is severely hampered by systemic and logistical challenges, with inadequate funding and infrastructure representing a primary barrier. Many early childhood education centers operate without the necessary physical resources, learning materials, and safe environments, which are fundamental for creating a conducive atmosphere for learning and for executing any curricular policies as intended (Adewusi et al., 2023). This chronic resource deficit, stemming from insufficient government investment and a over-reliance on private providers, directly impacts the quality of education and widens the gap between policy objectives on paper and the reality in classrooms, particularly in rural and low-income communities (Bello & Chumba, 2024). Furthermore, the success of any educational policy is critically dependent on a qualified teaching workforce, yet a significant impediment remains the shortage of professionally trained educators equipped to handle the unique developmental demands of early childhood education (Garba, 2024). This crisis in teacher quality is compounded by a general lack of instructional resources, leaving even the available educators without the tools or training needed to implement prescribed curricula and child-centered teaching methodologies effectively (Leigh et al., 2024). The confluence of these issues—inadequate infrastructure, insufficient funding, and a poorly resourced teaching workforce—creates a cycle of policy failure that stymies the development of a robust and effective pre-primary education system in Nigeria. 2.4.3 Should mother's tongue be used as language of instructions? A central and contentious policy challenge revolves around the language of instruction. The government's recent reversal of the mother-tongue policy was justified by citing declining academic outcomes in national examinations, arguing that an over-reliance on indigenous languages had negatively impacted student achievement, particularly in English. This stance is supported by research indicating that the mother tongue can influence performance in English, though other factors like teaching methods and resource shortages also play a critical role. Conversely, critics argue that the policy was abandoned prematurely without the necessary investment or time to prove its effectiveness. They contend that a shift to an English-only medium of instruction "risks stifling creativity, intelligence and the future of national innovation" by addressing a symptom rather than the root cause of educational failures (Hibbert, 2023). This perspective highlights a fundamental tension in Nigerian education between the pragmatic demands of global competitiveness, often associated with English proficiency, and the pedagogical benefits of mother-tongue education for cognitive development and cultural preservation. 3.1 The roles and perspectives of stakeholders in education The implementation of educational policies in Nigeria involves diverse stakeholders with competing priorities, which creates significant challenges for curriculum execution. Teachers, as the primary implementers, are often caught between policy requirements and classroom realities. They face inadequate support and resources, which can lead to apathy—a reaction stemming not from a lack of commitment but from systemic failures (Ogunyinka et al., 2015). A further complication is the inflexibility of a standardized national curriculum, which fails to account for the different regional conditions across Nigeria, causing implementation to break down where local contexts are not considered (Idowu, 2017). 3.1.1 Parental and Student priorities on Employability Parents and students often prioritize practical, economic outcomes over pedagogical theories, focusing on employability and success in high-stakes examinations. This pragmatic outlook explains why some parents support a shift to English-medium instruction, viewing English as a vital "global language" essential for future opportunity (Milligan & Tikly, 2016). This perspective is frequently driven by an instrumentalist view that links English proficiency directly to economic advancement, a powerful motivation that can override considerations of cultural preservation or mother-tongue education. 3.1.2 Government and Policy Maker Objectives Government bodies and policymakers often prioritize national economic development and global competitiveness when designing curricula. This can lead to the promotion of English-medium instruction based on an instrumentalist belief that it is a key driver of economic growth (Milligan & Tikly, 2016). However, this top-down approach frequently creates a disconnect, as policies are formulated without adequate consideration of the practical challenges of implementation, such as the readiness of teachers and the availability of resources in diverse local contexts (Erling et al., 2017). The core conflict in Nigerian education policy thus lies in the tension between these different stakeholder priorities. The government's focus on national development and global integration, often manifested in English-centric policies, clashes with the pedagogical evidence supporting mother-tongue education. Simultaneously, teachers' needs for practical support and adaptable curricula are often overlooked, creating a gap between policy intent and classroom reality. 4.0 Conclusion The analysis conclusively identifies a profound implementation gap within Nigeria's educational system, rooted not in curricular design but in entrenched systemic barriers. These include chronic underfunding, insufficient teacher development, technological deficits, and the complex linguistic realities that complicate policy execution. The contentious reversal of the mother-tongue instruction policy exemplifies the inherent tension between pedagogical ideals and practical constraints in a resource-limited, multilingual environment. Ultimately, this highlights a critical disconnect where well-intentioned policies are consistently undermined by a lack of supportive infrastructure, strategic capacity, and sustained investment, preventing the system from achieving its intended outcomes. 5.0 Recommendation To bridge this implementation gap, a multi-pronged, evidence-based strategy is imperative, beginning with a phased bilingual language policy that balances early-grade comprehension with global competitiveness through gradual transition models. This must be coupled with a comprehensive overhaul of teacher development, involving continuous professional training, competitive compensation, and direct involvement in curriculum processes to ensure practicality. Simultaneously, meeting UNESCO's funding benchmarks is essential to address critical resource shortages in infrastructure and teaching materials, while implementation frameworks must be decentralized to allow for contextual adaptation to Nigeria's diverse regional and linguistic landscapes, ensuring reforms are both effective and sustainable.
